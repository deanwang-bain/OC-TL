Opportunity Catalyst (OpCat) · Operating model

Who owns OpCat after the delivery partner steps back

A small Bain team, the people it needs, and three ways to time the handover. It follows from the post-GLS roadmap and the architecture the product already commits to.

Small team 20% build, 80% sustain as the working assumption 50/50 and 80/20 sized for comparison Structure and requirements only No budget No hiring timing Draft for Tech Lead sign-off
Operating assumption

The team sustains the product first and builds second

A team of the size the roadmap alone would need is not realistic inside Bain. This model uses a small team and accepts the price: new development runs at about a fifth of the team's effort, so the roadmap moves more slowly. The rest keeps what exists reliable. The next section sizes two other splits, 50/50 and 80/20 in favour of building, so the trade-off is visible.

80% sustain and support
20% build
The 80% coversLive data connections kept working and refreshed. Platform, access and security upkeep. AI quality: regression checks, evaluation sets and model changes. Partner support and issue triage. Corrections to calculations and methodology. Written decisions and runbooks.
The 20% buysOne roadmap initiative at a time, chosen for partner value. The next section proposes the order. Anything that needs two areas of the team at once moves slowly or waits.

How the numbers are estimated

  • Sustain floor. Keeping the product running needs a primary owner and a backup across six areas. At today's scope that is about 5.6 people's worth of effort, which is the 80% share of the seven-person team described below. This is a judgment, to be checked against the real support load.
  • Fixed across scenarios. The product has to be kept running whatever is being built, so the floor does not change. Team size is the floor divided by the sustain share.
  • Build pace yardstick. Sprint 3 burned about 370 logged hours in two weeks, roughly 4.6 people at 80 hours each. Build effort is compared with that to show how fast the roadmap could move.

The roadmap's effort has not been estimated, so these reaches are indicative. Build-first is a floor, not a forecast: more building means more to sustain and more coordination, so 28 understates it. A cheaper way to reach build-first pace is to keep the delivery partner building while a sustain-first Bain team owns the product. That is a different operating model and is not sized here.

Standards for the team as a whole

Capacity is ring-fencedAbout 20% of effort goes to building and about 80% to sustaining, planned in each sprint. Support does not quietly absorb the build share, and building does not starve support.
No single point of knowledgeEvery area has a named backup. Each critical component, such as the calculation engine, the provenance and event store, and the release pipeline, can be explained and changed by two people.
All senior, with a deep specialtyEvery hands-on role is seven or more years of experience and covers adjacent work. Nobody works in isolation from review.
Review has protected timeCode and design review are planned work. Delivery so far shows review queues, not build effort, holding items open for several sprints.
Data skill is in the core teamThe build phase to GLS ran without a data engineer. Live data is not sustained by a team that has none.
Evaluation can block a releaseThe evaluation sets belong to the AI and quality engineer and the data and methodology lead together. The tech and architecture director signs the release gate, so no one marks their own work.
The team controls its own accessPlatform access arrived mid-sprint in each of the last three sprints and set the pace of the work. The platform engineer owns provisioning, with a standing request in each sprint plan.
Decisions are written downArchitecture and data decisions have a record. Where policy pages are still empty, the team writes the standard before relying on it.

Recommendation: Scenario B. A small team cannot take every area at once and also carry 80% of the effort on support. Staging puts judgment-heavy ownership with Bain first and proves the team on real decisions before it takes on the engineering load. Scenario A is faster but fails if the team is thin. Scenario C is simplest to plan but leaves Bain a reviewer for the longest time. The Tech Lead makes the final call.

What has to be true at every step

  • Architecture decisions, runbooks and data lineage are written down and current before ownership of an area moves.
  • The receiving owner has run a release, an incident drill and a data refresh for the area at least once.
  • One backlog and one definition of done apply, whoever is doing the work.
  • The area's backup is named before the primary takes sole ownership.

Assumptions and gaps

  • The 20% build and 80% sustain split is the operating assumption for this model. It is a planning ratio and has not been measured against the team's real support load.
  • The sizing of all three scenarios rests on one judgment, a sustain floor of about 5.6 people. If the real floor is higher or lower, every team size moves with it.
  • The Sprint 3 yardstick assumes 80 logged hours per person per sprint. Logged hours were first recorded in that sprint and may understate the real effort, so the pace comparisons are rough.
  • The roadmap pages do not describe an ownership model, so the areas, roles and handover steps above are derived from the post-GLS initiatives, the design requirements and the architecture.
  • Security design, non-functional design and observability are still empty pages. The platform and security role scope is provisional until they are written.
  • The engineering lead backs up four areas, and the platform engineer and data lead each own several. A smaller team means more key-person risk, which the backup table reduces and does not remove.
  • The vision describes an ongoing portfolio-monitoring capability, and where it lives is undecided. It is not staffed here.
  • Serving Mainland China would need a separate instance of the platform, and is not staffed here.
