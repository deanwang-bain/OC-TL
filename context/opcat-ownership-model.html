Operating assumption

The team sustains the product first and builds second

A team of the size the roadmap alone would need is not realistic inside Bain. This model uses a small team and accepts the price: new development runs at about a fifth of the team's effort, so the roadmap moves more slowly. The rest keeps what exists reliable. This is the working assumption.

The team splits its effort evenly between building and sustaining

Half the effort keeps what exists reliable and half builds new capability. The roadmap moves at about the pace of Sprint 3, with a team of eleven. I would call this the upper end of what still reads as a small team, and that is a judgment you can overrule.

The team builds first and sustains second

Most of the effort goes to new development. The sustain effort is the same in absolute terms as in the other splits, so here it is a thin slice of a much larger team, and the first thing to be squeezed. This is the arithmetic result of building at nearly five times the Sprint 3 pace. It shows the cost of faster delivery and is not proposed. A team this size is unlikely to fit inside Bain.

How the numbers are estimated

  • Sustain floor. Keeping the product running needs a primary owner and a backup across six areas. At today's scope that is about 5.6 people's worth of effort. This is a judgment, to be checked against the real support load.
  • Fixed across splits. The product has to be kept running whatever is being built, so the floor does not change. Team size is the floor divided by the sustain share.
  • Build pace yardstick. Sprint 3 burned about 370 logged hours in two weeks, roughly 4.6 people at 80 hours each. Build effort is compared with that to show how fast the roadmap could move.

Build-first is a floor, not a forecast: more building means more to sustain and more coordination, so 28 understates it. A cheaper way to reach build-first pace is to keep the delivery partner building while a sustain-first Bain team owns the product. That is a different operating model and is not sized here.

Recommendation: Handover B. A small team cannot take every area at once and also carry 80% of the effort on support. A team of eleven can take areas faster than seven, but still not all at once while carrying half its effort on support. Even a team of twenty-eight cannot absorb every area at once, and a large team arriving together needs the most careful onboarding. Staging puts judgment-heavy ownership with Bain first and proves the team on real decisions before it takes on the engineering load. Handover A is faster but fails if the team is thin. Handover C is simplest to plan but leaves Bain a reviewer for the longest time. The Tech Lead makes the final call.

What has to be true at every step

  • Architecture decisions, runbooks and data lineage are written down and current before ownership of an area moves.
  • The receiving owner has run a release, an incident drill and a data refresh for the area at least once.
  • One backlog and one definition of done apply, whoever is doing the work.
  • The area's backup is named before the primary takes sole ownership.

Assumptions and gaps

  • The 20% build and 80% sustain split is the working assumption for this model. It is a planning ratio and has not been measured against the team's real support load.
  • The 50/50 split is a planning ratio and has not been measured against the team's real support load. Calling eleven the upper end of a small team is my judgment.
  • The 80/20 split is a reference point to show what faster delivery takes. It is a planning ratio and has not been measured against the real support load, and 28 is a floor.
  • The sizing rests on one judgment, a sustain floor of about 5.6 people. If the real floor is higher or lower, every team size moves with it.
  • The Sprint 3 yardstick assumes 80 logged hours per person per sprint. Logged hours were first recorded in that sprint and may understate the real effort, so the pace comparisons are rough.
  • The roadmap's effort has not been estimated, so what each split can reach is indicative.
  • The roadmap pages do not describe an ownership model, so the areas, roles and handover steps above are derived from the post-GLS initiatives, the design requirements and the architecture.
  • Security design, non-functional design and observability are still empty pages. The platform and security role scope is provisional until they are written.
  • The engineering lead backs up four areas, and the platform engineer and data lead each own several. A smaller team means more key-person risk, which the backup table reduces and does not remove.
  • The vision describes an ongoing portfolio-monitoring capability, and where it lives is undecided. It is not staffed here.
  • Serving Mainland China would need a separate instance of the platform, and is not staffed here.
