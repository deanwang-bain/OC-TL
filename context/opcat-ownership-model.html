Opportunity Catalyst (OpCat) · Operating model

Who owns OpCat after the delivery partner steps back

A team structure, the people it needs, and three ways to time the handover. Everything here follows from the post-GLS roadmap and the architecture the product already commits to.

Structure and requirements only No budget No hiring timing Draft for Tech Lead sign-off

What the team has to own

The post-GLS roadmap lists nine initiatives. They fall into six areas of ownership. The team is shaped around these areas, so each one has a named owner and a second person who can cover.

AreaRoadmap items that land hereWhy it needs a senior owner
Product and partner experienceOutput editing and deck refresh, refresh and go-back across an analysis, onboarding guide, the user-feedback streamPartners decide what goes in front of a client. Every change to the experience has to protect that judgment.
Calculation and methodologyBusiness-unit breakdown, calculation changes, lever taxonomy, sector KPIs, peer adjustmentsNumbers must be drillable to source, reasoning and confidence. Provenance cannot be added later.
Data and coverageLive data connections, further validation, additional sectors and geographies, the curated diagnostics libraryThe GLS demo runs on a curated data set, so live data is the largest unbuilt area.
AI and agentsNarrative and chat agents, evaluation gate, the learning pipeline from partner editsQuality of AI output is the product. It needs engineering and independent evaluation, not only prompts.
Platform, security and governanceScaling, cost efficiency, access, approval flows, real-time collaboration, confidential-data and regional rulesSecurity, non-functional and observability design are still unwritten, so judgment stands in for policy.
Bain content integrationCase studies, transformation stories, credentials and expert search surfaced inside the analysisNeeds people who know what good Bain content looks like and can say when a match is wrong.

Team structure

Three accountable leaders and five capability pods. Two of the leaders are directors, one for product and one for technology and architecture. Steady state is about 15 to 19 people. Each pod has a lead who owns quality for that area and at least one other person who can run it for a sprint without them.

Standards for the team as a whole

No single point of knowledgeTwo people can explain and change every critical component: the calculation engine, the provenance and event store, and the release pipeline.
Senior-weightedAt least half the engineers have seven or more years of experience. Juniors are paired, not isolated.
Review has its own capacityCode and design review are planned work, not spare time. Delivery so far shows review queues, not build effort, holding items open for several sprints.
A data engineer from the startThe build phase to GLS ran without one. Live data does not get built by a team that has none.
Evaluation is independentThe person who judges AI output does not report to the person who builds it.
Consulting-grade numeracy in every podAt least one person per pod has worked with the outputs a partner would present, and can tell when a number looks wrong.
The team controls its own accessPlatform access arrived mid-sprint in each of the last three sprints and set the pace of the work. The team owns provisioning, with a standing request in each sprint plan.
Decisions are written downArchitecture and data decisions have a record. Where policy pages are still empty, the team writes the standard before relying on it.

Three ways to time the handover

Timing is shown by phase, not by date. The phases follow the roadmap: a hardening period right after GLS, then the post-GLS initiatives in three waves, then steady state. The wave grouping is an assumption, because the post-GLS page lists the initiatives without ordering them.

PhaseWhat it covers
HardeningThe planned support and hardening period after GLS
Wave 1Technical foundations, scaling, cost efficiency
Wave 2Business-unit handling, data and coverage expansion
Wave 3Lifecycle and versioning, output editing, collaboration and governance
Steady stateThe Bain team owns and evolves the product
Bain-ledShared, with the delivery partnerDelivery partner-led

Scenario A: Early handover

The Bain team takes ownership at the end of hardening. The delivery partner supports while the Bain team leads the post-GLS work.

Fits when
The full team can be in place before post-GLS build starts, and the codebase is in a stable state after hardening.
Main risk
The team learns the system while the scope grows. A thin team here stalls the roadmap.
Exit test
The Bain team ships a release end to end, with the partner only answering questions.

Scenario B: Staged by capability

Recommended

Ownership moves in three steps, starting with the areas that carry judgment and least code: architecture, data and methodology. Engineering-heavy areas move last, once the team around them is proven.

Fits when
The team arrives in stages, and the roadmap cannot pause while it forms.
Main risk
Two operating models run side by side for a while, which needs clear boundaries and a single backlog.

Recommendation: Scenario B. It puts judgment-heavy ownership with Bain first, where the product's principles live, and it proves the team on real decisions before it takes on the engineering load. Scenario A is faster but fails if the team is thin. Scenario C is simplest to plan but leaves Bain a reviewer for the longest time. The Tech Lead makes the final call.

What has to be true at every step

  • Architecture decisions, runbooks and data lineage are written down and current before ownership of an area moves.
  • The receiving owner has run a release, an incident drill and a data refresh for the area at least once.
  • One backlog and one definition of done apply, whoever is doing the work.
  • The area's second person is named before the first one takes sole ownership.

Assumptions and gaps

  • The roadmap pages do not describe an ownership model, so the areas, roles and phases above are derived from the post-GLS initiatives, the design requirements and the architecture.
  • Security design, non-functional design and observability are still empty pages. The platform and security role scopes are provisional until they are written.
  • The vision describes an ongoing portfolio-monitoring capability, and where it lives is undecided. It is not staffed here.
  • Serving Mainland China would need a separate instance of the platform, and is not staffed here.
  • Team size is a range for planning the structure. It is not a hiring plan.
